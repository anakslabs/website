Every number on this site, and where it came from

Four of the figures on this website are other people's research, and every one of them links to the original. Three are our own field work, and we say exactly what we measured and on what. The rest belong to the specimen page, and the script that produced them is published.

The rule we hold ourselves to

A figure with no source is a decoration. Marketing pages in our industry are full of them — striking percentages with no study behind them, or with a study that says something narrower than the sentence quoting it. So we set one rule for this website: if we cannot link to the original publication, the number does not go on the page. Two figures we would have liked to use were removed under that rule rather than softened.

Everything below was checked by hand on 3 August 2026. If a link has since gone dead, tell us at help@anakslabs.com and we will fix or remove the figure.

Our own measurements

These three have no outside link to give you, because we produced them. So we tell you the sample definition instead — what we drew from, how many, and what counted as a finding. We hold the individual practices we looked at in confidence and do not name any of them.

The sample: 141 US clinic websites.

The base for both figures below, and referred to on the clinics page and the specimen page.

Drawn from
A random sample from the federal provider registry (NPPES, the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System — the public directory every US healthcare provider is listed in).
Size
141 US clinics. Where a clinic had a website, we opened it and read what the page hands to a search engine before anything on it runs.
Who ran it
Anaks Labs. Not commissioned, not peer-reviewed, not published as a paper. It is our own field work and we describe it as ours.
Limits
141 sites is enough to see a pattern and not enough to publish a national estimate. Treat these as what we found, not as what is true of every clinic in the United States.

72% of the US clinic sites we audited never answer a patient question on the page.

Appears on the clinics page and the specimen page.

Base
The clinic websites in the 141-clinic sample that had a site to read.
What counted
A page fails this if nowhere on it does it answer a question a patient would type — what it costs, whether an insurer is taken, how long recovery takes, whether you can be seen today, where to park. A phone number is not an answer.
Note
This one held at roughly the same level whether the site was well built or badly built. Good design does not protect against it.

1 in 3 of the US clinics we sampled had no website of their own at all.

Appears on the clinics page.

Base
All 141 clinics in the sample.
What counted
No site of the practice's own. A directory listing, a social page or a page on a hospital group's site is not a website the practice controls, and we counted those as none.

One figure we deliberately do not use

There is a number going round our industry at the moment: “59% of US adults use AI for health.” You will see it on agency pages and in pitch decks. It is a misreading of the survey we cite at the top of this page, and it is roughly twice the real figure.

In the West Health–Gallup study, 59% is a share of the people who already use AI for health — specifically, those who use it to prepare questions before a doctor's visit. The share of all US adults who have used AI for health information or advice is 25%. The two are not the same base, and swapping one for the other roughly doubles the claim.

We are pointing this out for a reason that is not generous. 25% is our number and it is the smaller one. We could have used 59% like everyone else, and the page would have hit harder. A figure you can check is worth more to you than a figure that flatters us, and the only way to prove we mean that is to show you where we chose the lower one.

The same rule removed two other figures we wanted. Conversion rates attributed to AI traffic, and the frequency with which AI summaries appear above search results, are both widely quoted and neither traces back to a primary study we could link. So neither appears anywhere on this site.

Corrections
